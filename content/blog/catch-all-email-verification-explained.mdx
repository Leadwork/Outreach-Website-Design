A catch-all domain may accept mail addressed to many local parts, including names that are not real mailboxes. That behavior makes address-level verification uncertain.

## Acceptance is not delivery

An SMTP response describes a step in a mail transaction. A positive response does not prove that a named employee has that mailbox, that the message will reach the inbox, or that a person will read it. Later filtering and delivery failures can still occur.

A 550 response is not conclusive proof that a mailbox does not exist: it can also indicate access or policy rejection. These distinctions are described in [RFC 5321](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc5321). Interpret the full response and context rather than one code in isolation.

## Treat vendor labels as classifications

Tools may label records deliverable, undeliverable, catch-all, risky, or unknown. The names and methods differ. Ask what a confidence score measures, when the check occurred, and whether the provider distinguishes temporary failures from invalid addresses.

Neither a second verifier nor a high confidence score removes uncertainty. Do not publish a universal catch-all accuracy percentage without a dated evaluation and a clear definition of accuracy.

## A practical list workflow

1. Deduplicate records and confirm the person’s current company and role from an appropriate source.
2. Verify the email and retain the check date and result category.
3. Separate invalid and uncertain records from the main delivery file.
4. Apply customer, opt-out, and other suppression rules across every export.
5. Agree with the buyer how uncertain records are handled, priced, and replaced.

If you evaluate vendors, use the same representative list and compare coverage, unknown results, costs, and observed failures. Record the methodology and limitations. A bounced message may reflect a policy decision rather than a nonexistent person, so avoid treating every bounce as definitive ground truth.
